From video to measure

Twenty-five seconds, measured word by word

Every plenary speech is broadcast and transcribed. We align the transcript to the recording word by word, so each word has a start and an end time. Within that interval we read the speaker’s head pose and hands from the video and the pitch and loudness of the voice from the audio.

This excerpt is from a speech by Frank Steffel (CDU/CSU) on 1 February 2019. He first addresses the Left, on his far left, and then names the AfD, on his far right. The playback stops at six steps that show what is measured, and what happens when the camera cuts away.

The panel beside the video lists what is recorded for the word being spoken. The chamber shows the head angle as a line from the speaker’s position.

The angle is a turn of the head toward a block of seats, not eye contact. The paper attributes an angle to a party only on the two flanks, where one group fills the view: the AfD from +45°, the Left from −53°. In the centre of the chamber several groups sit behind one another along any line of sight, so angles there are reported but not assigned to a party.

Does the measure work?

Naming a party turns the head toward it

For every word in the 19th Bundestag that names a parliamentary group other than the speaker’s own, we trace the speaker’s head angle from four seconds before to four seconds after the name. The summary above reports the average head angle during the first 1.5 seconds after the name.

If head orientation tracks whom a speaker addresses, naming the Left should turn the head left and naming the AfD should turn it right, with the parties in between falling in between. The averages fall in exact seating order.

Select a party to see the full trace around the naming word. The turn begins about a second before the name: speakers face the group they are about to address, then name it.

The averages sit well inside the flank sectors because speakers do not hold the turn for a whole sentence. They show the ordering; they do not make the centre of the chamber separable by angle. Each average is weighted by speaker, over several hundred speakers.

The design

How many AfD colleagues you get is a matter of arithmetic

The Bundestag divides detailed legislative work among permanent committees—for example on finance, health, or education. Each committee is a smaller version of parliament: its seats are distributed among the parliamentary groups in proportion to their strength in the full Bundestag.

After the 2017 election, the AfD held 92 of 709 Bundestag seats, or 13.0 percent, following a national vote share of 12.6 percent. The red line marks the 13.0 percent parliamentary benchmark that the committee allocation rule targets.

The staircase appears because committee seats cannot be divided. Whenever the committee gains one seat, the AfD’s proportional entitlement rises by about 0.13 of a seat, but its actual allocation must remain a whole number. It therefore stays unchanged until the next rounding threshold is crossed. A 27-seat committee gets three AfD members; a 28-seat committee gets four, producing the upward jump. The repeated threshold crossings—and the declining AfD share while its seat count remains fixed—create the staircase pattern.

Legislators chose their committees before the AfD arrived. How many AfD colleagues they then got followed from this arithmetic rather than from their own choice. Among the legislators we study, the resulting AfD share of committee colleagues ranges from 11.1 to 14.3 percent.

The outcome

What “public engagement” means

Public engagement with the AfD is a joint measure of two observable behaviors during plenary speeches: verbal engagement—whether the speech names the AfD—and visual attention—the share of the speech spent in sustained episodes with the speaker’s head turned toward the AfD flank.

The result

More AfD colleagues, less public engagement

We follow the same legislators before and after the AfD’s entry and compare those who ended up with more AfD committee colleagues to those with fewer. Moving from the least to the most exposed committee portfolio lowers this joint measure by 0.41 standard deviations. Both components fall, with the naming result being the more robust of the two.

Limits

Head orientation is a behavioral proxy, not eye contact, and is attributed to a party only on the two flanks. Estimates apply to legislators who kept at least one committee across the 2017 election, conditional on taking the floor, and are not statements about individual speakers.
